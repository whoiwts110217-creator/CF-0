# GRIM
## The Rules of the Game

*A short horror novel based on your character and backstory*

---

## PROLOGUE
### The One That Got Away

The first thing Grim learned about fear was that it made small things move faster.

A beetle could spend an entire afternoon beneath a stone, perfectly still, until a shadow crossed its back. Then its legs became a blur. A fly would throw itself against a window again and again, unable to understand why the world had suddenly turned solid. Spiders curled their legs inward when they could no longer run.

The boy watched all of it.

He was small then, with pale hair that never stayed combed. He liked the garden behind his mother's house because nobody bothered him there. He could sit in the dirt until the sunlight changed and study the tiny creatures that lived beneath the leaves.

At first, he only watched. Later, he began killing them.

He wasn't angry with the insects. He wasn't trying to punish them. He wanted to see what they would do when they realized they were in danger. Sometimes he laughed. Sometimes he tilted his head, puzzled by how quickly their movements stopped.

His mother found him one afternoon with a dead moth in his palm.

"That's disgusting," she said.

He looked up at her. "Why?"

"Because it is. Normal children don't do that."

He considered the answer and found it useless.

She struck him. He fell into the dirt, and the moth slipped from his hand. She struck him again when he didn't apologize.

"Cry," she snapped, as though tears would prove that he understood.

But he never did.

He felt pain. He knew what pain was. He simply did not give her the reaction she wanted, and that seemed to upset her more than anything else.

The next day he went back to the garden.

Once, a large beetle escaped him. He had it cornered against a cracked flowerpot when a voice called from the house. He turned his head for a second. By the time he looked back, the beetle was gone.

He searched beneath the flowerpot and among the weeds. Then he shrugged and found something else to do.

He would remember that beetle years later. Not because it haunted him, or because he regretted anything. It was simply something that had happened. A bug had escaped. The world had gone on.

As he grew older, the garden became too small for his curiosity.

And people, he discovered, made much more noise when they were afraid.

## CHAPTER ONE
### A Note in His Pocket

The street was almost empty when the person first noticed the footsteps.

Not loud footsteps. Not hurried ones. Just the slow, careless rhythm of someone with nowhere else to be.

Across the road, beneath a broken streetlight, a young man stood with his hands in his pockets.

His white hair was wild and frizzy, falling in thick, uneven strands over his eyes. Now and then, a dull red glimmer showed through it. His shirt was white, oversized and wrinkled, and his black pants hung loose around his legs. A small square of paper poked out of one pocket.

On the paper, written in black ink, was a single expression.

:)

He smiled as though he knew a joke nobody else had heard.

The corners of his mouth were marked by thin red lines. They ran outward like narrow slits, making his smile look a little too wide, a little too permanent. When he turned his head, the marks caught the light.

The person across the street looked away and kept walking.

Grim followed.

That wasn't the name the world knew him by. The world didn't know him by any name at all. No one had connected the missing people, the scattered rumors, and the cheerful little face he carried in his pocket. No one knew the pale stranger who drifted through the streets after dark.

He preferred it that way.

He had never bothered speaking to someone he didn't intend to kill. Ordinary conversation seemed pointless. People talked about weather, work, dinner, traffic. Grim was interested in what happened after their ordinary voices broke into screams.

He liked the chase best.

He liked the moment a person looked over their shoulder and realized they were being followed. He liked the sudden quickening of their steps. He liked how the street that had seemed familiar a minute earlier became strange and dangerous.

He liked to pretend it was a game.

To him, it really was one.

At the next corner, the person turned and found the young man standing much closer than before.

"Hello!" he said brightly.

His voice was playful, almost sing-song.

The person took a step back.

Grim's smile widened.

"Oh, good," he whispered. "You noticed."

The streetlight flickered. A car passed at the far end of the block. Then the road was empty again.

By the time the city woke the next morning, the person was gone.

And Grim had found someone new to play with.

## CHAPTER TWO
### The Rules Nobody Knows

The building had once been a children's theater.

Painted stars still decorated the ceiling. The ticket window was boarded shut. A faded sign in the hallway read PLEASE WALK, although there was no one left to enforce it.

Grim liked the place. It had a stage, long corridors, and doors that opened into rooms with no windows. When the wind passed through the broken roof, the old curtains rustled like an audience settling into its seats.

His captive sat on a chair beneath the stage lights.

They had been awake for a while. Their hands were trembling, but their face was strangely calm.

Grim paced in front of them, rocking on his heels.

"Okay!" he announced. "Here's the fun part."

He spread his arms toward the empty theater.

"You can run. You can hide. You can yell if you want. Most people yell." He laughed softly. "A lot."

The captive said nothing.

"I'll give you a head start," Grim continued. "That's fair, right?"

No answer.

He leaned closer. Beneath his white bangs, his red eyes shone faintly.

"Hey. Are you listening?"

"Yes."

Their voice was flat.

Grim blinked. He had expected a plea, an argument, maybe a desperate promise. Instead, the captive sounded as though someone had asked them to move seats on a bus.

"Then why aren't you scared?"

They stared at the dusty floor.

"I don't know."

The smile stayed on his face, but his voice lifted into a small, squeaky complaint.

"That's not an answer!"

He circled the chair once, thinking. He could tell them what he had done to others. He could show them the long hallway and tell them nobody had ever made it out. He could make the lights go dark.

But the person still didn't look frightened.

Grim felt something unfamiliar: not pity, not concern, but irritation at a game that refused to start.

"You know what happens if you lose?" he asked.

"Yes."

"And?"

The captive raised their head. "I didn't care whether I came home tonight."

For a moment, the theater was quiet enough to hear rain ticking against the roof.

Grim stopped moving.

He had met people who pretended to be brave. He had met people who tried to bargain. This was different. The person in front of him wasn't challenging him. They had simply reached a place in their life where danger no longer seemed to matter.

He didn't understand it.

"You want to die?" he asked.

They looked away. "I thought I did."

"Thought?"

"I don't know anymore."

Grim crouched in front of them. His hair hid his eyes completely.

"But if you're not afraid," he said, his voice growing thin and upset, "how am I supposed to know who's winning?"

For the first time, the captive looked directly at him.

"Maybe you aren't."

Grim's smile went still.

The game had changed, and nobody had asked his permission.

## CHAPTER THREE
### The Person With the Lantern

People called the other wanderer Moth.

Moth had short silver hair, sharp, watchful eyes, and an oversized dark coat with pockets full of folded paper. They carried a small lantern even on nights when the streetlights worked. The light was warm and steady, and it made their shadow stretch a long way behind them.

Moth had never been interested in frightening anyone.

They were interested in secrets.

When Moth was younger, someone in their neighborhood disappeared. The adults quickly settled on a comfortable explanation: the missing person had left on purpose. Nobody asked why their coat was still hanging by the door or why the last letter they had written was unfinished.

Moth had asked.

The adults told them to stop.

So Moth learned to keep questions to themself until the right moment. They learned that a locked door didn't necessarily hide a terrible truth, but that a confident answer could hide the fact that nobody had looked behind it.

Years passed. The missing person never returned.

Moth began writing down the names of people the city had stopped talking about.

Some were found. Some weren't. Each name stayed in the notebook until there was a real answer beside it.

Lately, the list had grown.

A delivery worker. A student. A man walking home from a late shift. Now another person, last seen on a quiet street beneath a broken lamp.

The disappearances seemed unconnected. Different neighborhoods, different days, different people.

But Moth had noticed one small thing: witnesses sometimes remembered a young man with pale hair. Never clearly. Never for long. He was just someone standing nearby, smiling at nothing.

No one had spoken to him.

Moth closed the notebook and lifted the lantern.

At the edge of town stood the old children's theater, a building the city had forgotten. A light flickered inside, though its electricity had supposedly been cut off years ago.

Moth watched it for a long time.

Then they took out their phone, told someone where they were going, and stepped through the broken gate.

The lantern swung gently at their side.

Somewhere inside, a voice was laughing.

## CHAPTER FOUR
### Something Interesting

"Let's try talking," Grim said.

The captive looked at him.

He had dragged a second chair onto the stage and sat in it backward, resting his chin on the backrest. His black pants pooled around his shoes. The folded smiley-face note was still tucked into his pocket.

"Usually I don't do this part for very long," he explained. "People are always too busy screaming."

"Then why talk to me?"

"Because you're being weird."

The captive almost smiled at that.

Grim noticed. "Oh! Was that funny?"

"A little."

He clapped once, delighted. "See? You're getting better!"

They looked past him at the painted stars on the ceiling.

"Did you always do this?"

"Play games?"

"Hurt things."

Grim considered the question. His voice softened, not with regret but with the distant fondness of someone remembering an old hobby.

"Bugs, when I was little. I liked watching them move."

"Did any get away?"

"One big one."

"Do you still think about it?"

"Sometimes."

"Does it bother you?"

"Nope!"

He answered so quickly that the captive believed him.

"My mom hated it," Grim added. "She said it was weird. She used to hit me. Wanted me to cry, I think."

"Did you?"

"Never."

He said it without pride, without sadness. It was only a fact.

The captive studied the thin red lines at the corners of his mouth. His hair kept most of his face hidden, but his smile was always visible.

"Do you think everything is a game?" they asked.

"Everything fun."

"And if someone doesn't want to play?"

His voice went higher. "They have to! That's the whole point!"

"Why?"

"Because I said so."

The captive looked at him for a long time.

"If you need me to be afraid," they said, "doesn't that mean I'm the one deciding whether you win?"

Grim went quiet.

The old theater creaked around them.

Then he began to laugh. Not the laugh he used to scare people. A genuine, delighted sound.

"Oh, I like that," he said.

He leaned forward until his pale hair nearly brushed their face.

"You can call me Grim."

The captive blinked. "Grim?"

"Mhm! Don't tell everyone. It's a special prize."

They had no idea whether knowing his name made them safer or placed them in greater danger.

Grim sat back, smiling.

"Now," he said, "say something else interesting."

## CHAPTER FIVE
### Moth at the Door

Moth found the first painted smile in the theater's entrance hall.

It had been drawn on the wall with a black marker: two dots and a curved line. Childish. Ordinary. It would have been easy to ignore if the building hadn't been so silent.

The second smile was on an old ticket stub. The third was scratched into the paint beside a doorway.

Moth wrote nothing down. There would be time for notes later.

They followed the sound of voices past a row of cracked mirrors and into the back of the auditorium.

Through a gap in the curtain, Moth saw the captive sitting onstage and a white-haired stranger lounging in a chair across from them.

The stranger was laughing.

Moth had expected shouting or threats. The laughter was worse, because it sounded so ordinary.

They took one step forward.

A floorboard groaned.

The white-haired man stopped laughing.

"Ooooh," he said. "Did somebody come to watch?"

Moth stepped into the aisle, lantern raised.

"I'm here for them," Moth said, nodding toward the captive.

The stranger tilted his head. His long bangs slipped farther across his face.

"You don't even know if they want to leave."

Moth looked at the captive, not at him.

"Do you?" Moth asked.

The captive opened their mouth, then closed it again.

It was a harder question than it should have been.

The white-haired stranger stood. His smile widened, the red lines at its corners catching the stage light.

"Wait, wait!" he chirped. "We haven't finished the first game!"

Moth's grip tightened around the lantern handle.

"Who are you?"

The stranger's voice dropped into a teasing whisper. "Wouldn't you like to know?"

Moth didn't answer. They were watching the captive's hands, the distance to the aisle, the way the stranger moved when someone spoke.

The stranger took a step closer.

"You're not scared either?" he asked.

"I am," Moth said.

That answer surprised him.

"Then why'd you come in?"

"Because being afraid isn't the same as leaving someone alone."

For a second, Grim looked almost thoughtful.

Then he giggled.

"Two players," he said. "That's new."

The captive finally spoke.

"Don't. They didn't do anything."

Grim turned back toward them.

"Awww," he said in a high, wounded voice. "You're worried about somebody now? I thought you didn't care!"

The captive stared at Moth's lantern.

It was such a small light, but someone had carried it into the dark for them.

They hadn't expected anyone to come.

## CHAPTER SIX
### The Question at the End

The theater seemed to shrink around the three of them.

Grim moved to the center of the stage, spreading his arms as if he were introducing the final act of a show. The painted stars above him were peeling. The audience seats were empty. Rain whispered through the damaged roof.

"Okay," he announced. "New rules!"

Moth said nothing.

"You both get to talk. You can ask questions. You can say whatever you want." His smile grew sharper. "But if I don't like what you say, the game ends badly."

His voice was cheerful. He wasn't pretending. That was what frightened Moth most: the pleasure in his face was real.

"And what happens if you like the answer?" Moth asked.

Grim shrugged. "Maybe something fun!"

"You don't know, do you?"

The playful voice rose into a squeak. "I know the rules!"

"You keep changing them."

"Because it's my game!"

The captive looked between them. Earlier that night, they had felt empty enough to walk through the city without caring whether they returned. That emptiness hadn't disappeared. They were still exhausted. They still didn't know how to explain what had brought them here.

But now there was Moth, standing in the aisle with a lantern, and there was a tomorrow they had not yet seen.

The captive realized they wanted the chance to see it.

That didn't fix everything. It didn't erase the pain that had made them indifferent to danger. It was only one small, stubborn wish.

Still, it was theirs.

"Grim," they said.

He turned immediately, delighted to hear his name.

"Yes?"

"I have a question."

"Oh, I love questions."

"If I wanted to die when you found me, but I don't want to now... did you win, or did you lose?"

For the first time all night, Grim didn't answer.

Moth could hear footsteps somewhere beyond the theater walls. Help might be coming. Or the sound might only be the old building settling in the rain.

Grim looked from the captive to Moth and back again. His white hair hid his eyes, but a faint red light moved beneath the strands.

Then he laughed softly.

"That's a really good question," he said.

His hand brushed the little note in his pocket.

:)

He had always known how his games were supposed to end.

Now, with two people watching him and an answer he hadn't expected, he had to decide what kind of game this was.

The captive waited.

Moth held the lantern steady.

And Grim, smiling as though the world had just told him its funniest joke, opened his mouth to speak.

**END OF BOOK ONE**

## CREATOR'S NOTES

**Grim's established character details:** Wild white frizzy hair that covers his red eyes; oversized white shirt; baggy black pants; thin red slit-like marks extending from his smile; a pocket note reading `:)`; a genuinely playful, smug personality; a voice that becomes squeaky and upset when someone doesn't react with fear. As a child, he killed bugs out of fascination, was beaten by his mother for it, and never cried. One bug escaped, and he remembers it without attaching special meaning to it. As an adult, he becomes a serial killer, kidnaps a passerby, and treats his victims' fear and attempts to run as a game. He has never spoken to someone he did not intend to kill. He reveals the name Grim only when someone says something that interests him. One captive surprises him by showing no emotion and revealing that they had wanted to die. What he ultimately does depends on what they say.

**Additional story elements introduced for this book:** The abandoned children's theater, the missing-person investigation, and Moth's personal history are new fictional details. Moth is the silver-haired, lantern-carrying character introduced alongside Grim. The captive remains unnamed, and their ultimate fate is deliberately left open for you to decide.
